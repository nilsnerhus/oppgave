5  Methods

To empirically examine whether adaptation discourse reflects epistemological control, I develop a mixed methods approach that combines computational text analysis with auto-ethnographic reflection, enabling analysis of both the structural patterns and cultural processes that shape expert knowledge production.

This research addresses questions about epistemological control and expert authority that require methodological innovation beyond traditional single-method approaches. Examining whether expert systems produce genuine knowledge diversity or systematic convergence demands both systematic analysis of expert discourse across contexts and critical understanding of how this discourse is produced within expert cultures. The methodology therefore integrates computational analysis of National Adaptation Plans with reflexive analysis of my own positioning within expert governance systems, using climate adaptation as the empirical case for testing broader theories of control.

The mixed methods design reflects both practical and theoretical considerations. Practically, examining epistemological control requires analysis across multiple scales—from individual expert socialization to institutional discourse patterns to global knowledge convergence. Theoretically, understanding how control operates through expert systems requires insight into both the structural outcomes (discourse homogenization) and the cultural processes (expert reproduction) that produce these outcomes. Neither computational nor ethnographic analysis alone could adequately address questions about how expert authority operates as both knowledge system and cultural reproduction.

My sustained engagement with climate governance provides unique analytical access to expert cultures while raising important methodological and ethical questions about studying systems from within. Rather than treating this insider positioning as limitation requiring control, I employ auto-ethnographic methods that use reflexive analysis as analytical resource. This approach enables examination of how expert systems shape both policy outputs and expert subjectivities, revealing connections between institutional patterns and individual experiences of expert socialization within systems of control.

The chapter proceeds through three methodological discussions. First, I detail the computational text analysis approach, explaining how structural topic modeling and the Control Index enable systematic examination of discourse convergence patterns across expert-produced policy documents. Then I examine the auto-ethnographic component, discussing how my trajectory through expert systems provides analytical insight into how epistemological control operates through cultural reproduction. Finally, I address the integration of these approaches, explaining how mixed methods analysis enables examination of expert governance as both structural discourse and lived cultural practice.

This methodological combination serves the broader theoretical project of understanding how expert systems operate as mechanisms of epistemological control. The computational analysis reveals the homogenization that characterizes expert knowledge production, while auto-ethnographic analysis illuminates how expert cultures reproduce the assumptions and practices that generate this homogenization. Together, these approaches enable analysis of climate adaptation not just as policy framework but as exemplar of how expert authority shapes whose knowledge counts and which futures are imaginable. Climate adaptation becomes the empirical window through which broader patterns of expert control can be examined and understood.

5.1 Auto-Ethnography

This research draws on my sustained engagement with climate governance as both participant and analyst, using auto-ethnographic methods to examine the cultural logics of climate expertise from within.

My analysis of climate adaptation discourse is informed by a decade of participation in Norwegian climate policy processes, environmental organizations, and international climate governance forums. This positioning provides unique analytical access to the cultural assumptions, institutional practices, and epistemological frameworks that shape climate expertise, but it also requires methodological transparency about how this insider knowledge informs my interpretation of the NAP corpus.

Auto-ethnography offers established methods for analyzing cultural systems from within (ellis2011?). Rather than claiming objective distance from my research subject, this approach recognizes that my socialization into climate expertise provides both analytical resources and potential blind spots. The method involves systematic reflection on personal experience within cultural contexts, using this reflection to illuminate broader social processes (spry2001?).

My engagement with climate change began during secondary school and continued through roles in environmental organizations, policy advisory positions, and international climate forums. I have always seen myself as a climate activist, and I believe this trajectory reflects the typical path of climate expert formation - university education in environmental issues, participation in advocacy organizations, and eventual professional integration into structrued policy processes.

A methodological turning point for me came through exposure to decolonial theory during studies at The New School in New York city. This theoretical framework provided tools for analyzing the very knowledge systems I had been trained within, revealing how what appeared as neutral expertise embedded particular epistemological assumptions and power relations. Here, the problematization of knowledge as a form of control, and the realities of different ontoepistemological worlds became a central academic interest for me (Escobar 1995; Whyte 2018; Tuck and Yang 2012)

This enabled a form of “reflexive distance”, where I got the tools to analyze my own (knowledge) systems from both insider and critical perspectives (davies1999?). My continued participation in climate governance provides ongoing access to expert logics and institutional practices, while my anthropological training enables systematic analysis of these cultural patterns.

The auto-ethnographic component illuminates several aspects of climate governance that pure discourse analysis might miss. The separation between knowledge production, communication, and policy implementation appears natural from within expert cultures but becomes visible as a constructed division when analyzed reflexively. Similarly, the dismissal of community knowledge as “not representative” - something I have experienced directly when serving as an elected representative - reveals how expert systems maintain authority by controlling definitions of legitimate knowledge.

This methodological approach follows established anthropological practices of “studying up” (nader1972?) and analyzing elite institutions from insider positions. It also connects to recent work on institutional ethnography that examines how professional cultures shape policy processes (smith2005?). The combination of computational discourse analysis and reflexive ethnography enables analysis of both the textual products of climate governance (NAPs) and the cultural processes that produce these texts.

5.2 Computational Text Analysis

To empirically examine whether adaptation discourse reflects epistemological diversity or monoculture, I develop a computational approach centered around structural topic modeling and the “Control Index”—a measurement tool for quantifying discourse centralization in policy documents.

Computational text analysis offers powerful tools for examining large corpora of policy documents that would be impossible to analyze manually (grimmer2013?; monroe2008?). Within climate governance research, computational approaches enable systematic comparison across countries and over time, revealing patterns in how climate challenges are understood and addressed (Wright et al. 2023; Schoenfeld et al. 2018).

The analysis employs established UN categorization systems to examine whether discourse patterns correspond to expected geographic and economic factors. Since climate change represents fundamentally geographical vulnerability—with impacts varying by location, elevation, coastline exposure, and ecosystem type—we might expect countries facing similar physical challenges to develop similar adaptation approaches. The analysis therefore examines discourse patterns across UN categories including income levels, geographic regions, and climate vulnerability classifications to test whether adaptation planning reflects climate-responsive diversity or institutional convergence.

The analysis examines all English-language National Adaptation Plans submitted to the UNFCCC, representing official government statements about climate adaptation priorities and making them ideal for analyzing how states translate international climate frameworks into national contexts. Corpus preparation involved standardization steps to ensure comparability across documents with different formatting and organization, including conversion to plain text and standard preprocessing while preserving climate-specific technical terminology.

Structural topic modeling provides a probabilistic approach to identifying latent themes within large text corpora while accounting for document-level covariates (roberts2014?; Roberts, Stewart, and Tingley 2019b). Unlike traditional topic modeling approaches that assume documents are exchangeable, STM allows investigation of how document metadata—such as country income level, geographic vulnerability, or regional affiliation—influences topic prevalence. This makes STM particularly appropriate for policy research where institutional context shapes discourse patterns. The modeling process involved determining the optimal number of topics through coherence and exclusivity metrics (mimno2011?). Coherence measures how semantically related the words within each topic are, while exclusivity captures how distinctive topics are from each other.

The entire analysis was conducted in R (R Core Team 2025), with reproducible research principles supported by renv (Ushey and Wickham 2025) for package management and Quarto for document generation. Data manipulation and transformation relied on the tidyverse ecosystem (Wickham et al. 2019), while the analytical pipeline employed the STM package (Roberts, Stewart, and Tingley 2019a) for all topic modeling calculations except the Control Index, which represents my methodological contribution. All code and data processing steps are documented and available for replication on GitHub. Each topic is characterized by its most frequent and exclusive (FREX) terms, which identify words that appear both commonly within the topic and distinctively compared to other topics (bischof2012?). This dual criterion helps ensure that topics capture meaningful thematic distinctions rather than general vocabulary, and each topic is named by the topiclabels package (Rieger et al. 2024).

The Control Index is an attempt to quantify the degree to which discourse concentrates around a small number of dominant topics rather than distributing attention across the full thematic range. This measure addresses a crucial limitation in existing topic modeling applications to policy research, which typically examine topic prevalence but not discourse centralization patterns. The index calculation involves extracting the average topic distribution for each analytical category, identifying the most prominent topics within each category, and calculating what proportion of total discourse these dominant topics represent. This captures how much of each group’s adaptation discourse focuses on key themes versus being distributed across many different topics.

To enable comparison across different categorization schemes, all Control Index values are normalized to a 0-1 scale, where 0 represents perfectly distributed discourse and 1 represents complete concentration on the dominant topics. Higher values indicate that most of the group’s adaptation discourse focuses on a small number of topics, suggesting potentially constrained understanding of adaptation challenges. Lower values indicate more distributed attention across the full range of adaptation topics, potentially reflecting greater epistemological diversity.

Establishing whether Control Index patterns are statistically meaningful requires hypothesis testing using STM’s regression capabilities (roberts2014?). For each country grouping, I test whether categorical membership significantly influences the distribution of dominant topics, treating topic proportions as dependent variables and group membership as predictor variables. The statistical tests employ binary indicators for each category to examine specific hypotheses about discourse convergence, with standard significance testing providing evidence about whether observed patterns reflect systematic differences or random variation.

This computational approach builds on recent advances in political science and international relations that use large-scale text analysis to examine policy discourse (grimmer2013?; lucas2015?). Within climate governance research, computational methods have revealed important patterns in how countries frame climate issues in international forums (Wright et al. 2023), how urban climate narratives converge over time (Westman, Castán Broto, and Huang 2023), and how scientific uncertainty is communicated in climate assessments (petersen2019?). However, computational text analysis faces several important limitations that shape how findings should be interpreted. Topic modeling assumes that texts can be meaningfully decomposed into discrete themes, which may not capture the complex intertextuality and contextual meanings that characterize policy discourse (mohr2013?).

The method privileges frequently occurring terms and may miss subtle but theoretically important variations in how concepts are used. The Control Index, while providing useful quantification of discourse patterns, cannot capture qualitative differences in how similar topics are approached. Two countries might both emphasize agricultural adaptation but frame it through completely different epistemological lenses—one through market-based efficiency and another through food sovereignty. The quantitative measure would show convergence where critical analysis might reveal fundamental divergence.

The temporal dimension presents another analytical challenge. While all NAPs in the corpus were submitted by 2023, they represent different stages in adaptation planning processes across countries. Some documents reflect initial adaptation assessments while others represent updates to existing strategies. This temporal variation may influence topic patterns in ways the analysis cannot fully account for.

Despite these limitations, computational text analysis provides the best capabilities for understanding climate governance at scale. The method enables systematic comparison across contexts that would be impossible through traditional qualitative approaches alone. When combined with critical theoretical frameworks and reflexive methodology, computational analysis can reveal patterns of convergence and exclusion that illuminate how climate governance operates as a global system of knowledge production and political control.

5.3 Mixed Methods

The integration of computational discourse analysis and auto-ethnographic reflection enables examination of climate governance from both systemic and experiential perspectives, revealing how institutional patterns connect to lived realities of expert culture and practice.

Mixed methods research combines different methodological approaches to address research questions that cannot be adequately answered through single-method designs (creswell2017?; tashakkori2010?). Within policy research, mixed methods approaches have proven particularly valuable for examining how macro-level institutional patterns connect to micro-level practices and experiences (yanow2006?). The combination of quantitative and qualitative methods enables researchers to identify broad patterns while understanding the cultural processes that produce these patterns (small2011?).

This research employs what mixed methods scholars term a “concurrent embedded” design, where qualitative insights inform the interpretation of quantitative findings rather than operating as separate analytical tracks (creswell2017?). The computational analysis of NAP discourse provides systematic evidence about convergence patterns across climate governance, while auto-ethnographic reflection illuminates the cultural logics and institutional practices that produce this convergence. Neither method alone could adequately address the research questions about epistemological diversity and expert authority in climate adaptation.

The computational approach reveals the “what” of climate governance—patterns of discourse centralization that span countries with vastly different climate challenges and vulnerabilities. However, quantitative analysis cannot explain why these patterns emerge or how they are sustained across different national contexts. Auto-ethnographic analysis provides insight into the “how” of expert culture reproduction—the socialization processes, institutional pressures, and cultural assumptions that shape how climate expertise operates in practice (ellis2011?; anderson2006?).

The integration strategy follows established principles for mixed methods policy research that emphasize complementarity rather than convergence (greene2007?). The two approaches examine climate governance at different analytical scales and through different epistemological lenses, with computational analysis revealing structural patterns and ethnographic analysis illuminating cultural processes. This complementarity enables examination of how individual experiences of expert socialization connect to broader institutional patterns of knowledge production and political control.

The temporal dimension provides crucial integration between the methodological approaches. The computational analysis captures adaptation discourse at a specific moment when international climate frameworks have stabilized around particular approaches to planning and implementation. The auto-ethnographic analysis provides longitudinal perspective on how these frameworks emerged and became naturalized within expert communities during the same period. This temporal complementarity enables analysis of both the current state of climate governance and the historical processes that produced this state.

Methodological tensions between the approaches require explicit acknowledgment and analytical strategy (johnson2007?). Computational analysis assumes that meaningful patterns can be identified through statistical aggregation across contexts, while auto-ethnographic analysis emphasizes the situated, contextual, and non-generalizable aspects of cultural experience. Rather than resolving this tension, the research uses it productively by allowing each approach to illuminate the limitations of the other. Computational findings about discourse convergence gain depth through ethnographic insight into how expert cultures produce standardization, while ethnographic observations about institutional pressures gain empirical grounding through quantitative evidence of their effects.

The mixed methods approach also addresses what policy researchers identify as the “implementation gap” between formal policy frameworks and actual practices (lipsky2010?). NAPs represent official adaptation discourse, but auto-ethnographic analysis provides insight into how this discourse operates within the organizational cultures that produce and implement adaptation policies. This combination enables examination of both the textual products of climate governance and the social processes that create these texts.

The ethical considerations of combining computational and ethnographic approaches require careful attention to different standards of consent and representation (tracy2010?). The computational analysis examines public documents where consent issues do not arise, while the auto-ethnographic component focuses on publicly observable processes and personal transformation rather than analysis of non-consenting individuals. This ethical boundary enables integration of different forms of knowledge while respecting the limitations of each approach.

This mixed methods design ultimately enables analysis of climate governance as both discourse and culture, revealing how patterns identified through computational analysis connect to the lived realities of expert practice and institutional reproduction. The combination provides analytical resources for understanding climate adaptation not just as policy output but as a cultural system that shapes how communities can respond to environmental change.
