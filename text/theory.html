4  Theory

This chapter develops a theory of control that explains how expert communities manage both knowledge and futures through planning processes that transform political questions into technical problems, eliminating alternative ways of knowing and being while appearing to be inclusive and participatory.

A fundamental question shapes contemporary global governance: who makes decisions about the future? This question becomes particularly urgent in the context of environmental change, where planning processes claim to anticipate and prepare for future challenges while potentially shaping what futures become possible. The seemingly neutral technical character of much contemporary planning may obscure deeper questions about whose knowledge counts, whose visions matter, and whose interests are served by particular approaches to future-making.

The stakes of this question extend beyond academic analysis to practical questions of democracy and justice. If planning processes actively create the futures they claim to predict, then examining these processes becomes essential for understanding how power operates through seemingly neutral technical arrangements (Ferguson 1994). When we plan to continue extracting oil, we end up extracting oil. When we plan for technological solutions to environmental problems, we create worlds organized around technology rather than other possible arrangements. The future becomes “acted” rather than simply predicted, making planning documents into blueprints for particular kinds of worlds.

Understanding how this dynamic operates requires examining the role of expert communities in contemporary governance. Expert communities claim authority through technical competence and scientific objectivity, positioning themselves as neutral arbiters capable of managing complex challenges (jasanoff2004?). Yet expert communities are also social formations with particular ways of seeing, specific professional interests, and strong incentives for conformity and peer recognition (kuhn1962?). When expert communities encounter phenomena that cannot be understood through their frameworks, the problem lies with the phenomena rather than the framework - they become inefficient, traditional, illegal, or simply wrong (Scott 1998).

This dynamic creates what can be understood as “technical rendering” - the transformation of complex social and political realities into simplified representations that can be managed through expert tools (Li 2007). Technical rendering operates through abstraction, quantification, and standardization, systematically excluding aspects of reality that cannot be captured through these processes while appearing to create objective knowledge (porter1995?). This contrasts with approaches that recognize complexity and specificity through detailed, contextual understanding (geertz1973?).

The implications of technical rendering extend beyond questions of knowledge to questions of power and possibility. When political questions about desired futures become technical problems requiring expert solutions, the range of possible responses narrows dramatically (Ferguson 1994). Alternative ways of understanding challenges and appropriate responses become literally unthinkable within expert frameworks, creating what might be termed comprehensive mechanisms for managing both knowledge and futures (Santos 2016).

4.1 Anticipatory Governance

This section explores how anticipatory governance may operate as a mechanism for controlling futures rather than enabling them, suggesting that it transforms political questions about desired futures into technical problems that can only be solved by experts.

Anticipatory governance emerged in the early 2000s as governments and institutions sought better ways to manage complex, emerging challenges before they became unmanageable. The framework is defined as “a broad-based capacity extended through society that can act on a variety of inputs to manage emerging knowledge-based technologies while such management is still possible” (Guston 2014). It emphasizes building three core capacities: foresight (anticipating future developments), engagement (involving stakeholders in planning), and integration (coordinating across institutions and knowledge systems).

The appeal is clear. Rather than reacting to problems after they manifest, anticipatory governance promises to identify and address challenges while there remains time to shape outcomes (Guston 2014). This forward-looking orientation could create space for diverse communities to participate in shaping their own futures. The emphasis on engagement suggests inclusive processes, foresight implies systematic attention to possibilities, and integration promises coordination across different knowledge systems.

Yet examining anticipatory governance in practice reveals a fundamental tension. The framework appears to rest on understanding the future as something that can be known, predicted, and managed through expert analysis and systematic planning (beck1992?; giddens1990?). This may transform political questions about what kind of world communities want into technical problems about efficiently managing projected changes (winner1980?). The future risks becoming something that happens to people rather than something they create through their actions.

A crucial insight emerges when recognizing that planning processes do not simply predict futures but actively create them (austin1962?; butler1990?). When documents designate areas as future risk zones, this designation becomes performative (Paprocki 2019). Investment shifts away, infrastructure deteriorates, and outmigration accelerates, creating the very abandonment that planning supposedly aimed to prevent (watts2013?). Planning creates the futures it claims to predict rather than responding to inevitable developments.

This performative dimension reveals how anticipatory governance may operate through future control rather than future response. The framework appears to privilege certain epistemological approaches to understanding time and change (Inayatullah 1990). The “predictive” approach relies on scientific forecasting, scenario planning, and risk assessment. The “cultural” approach recognizes that communities may have different ways of understanding time, emphasizing relationships and cyclical temporalities (cajete2000?; deloria1973?). The “critical” approach examines whose interests are served by particular future visions (Inayatullah 1990).

The predictive approach dominates contemporary governance frameworks through its promise of scientific objectivity and technical precision (jasanoff2004?; porter1995?). This approach employs sophisticated tools including climate models that project temperature and precipitation changes decades into the future, economic models that calculate costs and benefits of different interventions, and vulnerability assessments that rank populations according to exposure to environmental hazards (beck1992?; douglas1992?). These tools appear neutral and scientific, lending legitimacy to planning processes by suggesting that future challenges can be objectively assessed and efficiently managed.

Yet the cultural approach reveals fundamentally different ways of relating to time and change that challenge the assumptions of predictive planning (cajete2000?; deloria1973?). Indigenous communities often organize temporality around relationships with ancestors and future generations, creating responsibilities that extend across many generations rather than the short timeframes of policy cycles (berkes2012?; kimmerer2013?). Traditional ecological knowledge systems may emphasize cyclical patterns, seasonal rhythms, and reciprocal relationships with non-human beings rather than linear projections and technological control (berkes2012?; turnbull2000?). These alternative temporalities generate different understandings of appropriate responses to environmental change, often emphasizing adaptation through relationship and reciprocity rather than prediction and control.

The critical approach adds another dimension by examining how future visions serve particular interests while marginalizing others (Inayatullah 1990; harvey2000?). This perspective reveals that seemingly neutral planning tools embed particular values and assumptions about what constitutes legitimate knowledge, appropriate development, and desirable futures (winner1980?; feenberg1991?). Cost-benefit analyses, for instance, systematically privilege economic efficiency over social cohesion, cultural continuity, or ecological integrity (o?’neill1993; martinez-alier2002?). Scenario planning exercises may present certain development pathways as inevitable rather than as choices shaped by power relations and competing interests (slaughter1996?; Inayatullah 2013).

However, the tools employed in anticipatory governance may systematically favor predictive approaches while marginalizing cultural and critical alternatives. Climate models and economic projections typically begin from current trajectories, making it difficult to imagine transformative change (jasanoff2004?; porter1995?). Cost-benefit analyses may discount future impacts in ways that undervalue long-term transformation (maccormick2007?). Risk assessments might focus on exposure to hazards within existing arrangements rather than questioning the relations that create vulnerability (dean1999?; douglas1992?).

This systematic privileging suggests that planning tools may eliminate possibilities through “defuturing” - the destruction of futures through design decisions and institutional arrangements that eliminate alternatives before they emerge (Fry 2019; virilio1986?). Planning frameworks may create “radical monopoly” over processes of imagination, constraining how communities can think about and respond to challenges (Illich 2009; winner1980?). The proliferation of assessment frameworks and modeling systems might monopolize imagination rather than expanding it (feenberg1991?; barry2001?).

The implications extend beyond technical planning to fundamental questions of democracy and self-determination (barber1984?; pateman1970?). When communities cannot articulate their own visions but must translate understanding into expert frameworks to be heard, anticipatory governance may function as temporal colonialism (said1978?; chakrabarty2000?). The future becomes colonized by present power relations before communities can create it themselves.

This temporal colonialism operates through what can be understood as the professionalization of future-making (larson1977?; abbott1988?). Planning becomes the domain of certified experts who possess the technical knowledge necessary to navigate complex assessment frameworks and modeling systems (mitchell2002?; Ferguson 1994). Communities facing environmental challenges find themselves dependent on consultants and technical advisors who can translate their experiences into the vocabulary required by funding institutions (mosse2005?; Li 2007). The effect is not simply exclusion but the transformation of political subjects into clients of expert services.

The three capacities of anticipatory governance - foresight, engagement, and integration - may thus operate as mechanisms for incorporating diverse perspectives into predetermined frameworks rather than enabling genuinely alternative futures (Li 2007; cruikshank1999?). Foresight becomes monopolized by those with access to sophisticated modeling tools. Engagement becomes consultation about expert-designed options rather than support for community-generated visions. Integration becomes coordination of expert perspectives rather than bridging between expert and community knowledge systems (jasanoff2004?; wynne1992?).

The framework may also create what can be understood as “anticipatory capture” - the absorption of future possibilities into present institutional arrangements (deleuze1992?; hardt2000?). Rather than opening space for transformation, anticipatory governance may extend current power relations into the temporal dimension, ensuring that future possibilities remain compatible with existing interests (harvey2005?; brenner2004?). The promise of managing emerging challenges becomes a mechanism for preventing challenges from emerging to existing arrangements.

4.2 Epistemology

This section examines how certain knowledge systems achieve dominance while others are systematically marginalized, exploring the mechanisms through which expert communities claim universal validity while rendering alternatives invisible or illegitimate.

Understanding epistemological dynamics in global governance requires confronting how European-derived categories achieved dominance through colonial expansion and continue to operate as universal frameworks. The concept of “provincializing Europe” reveals how categories like the scientific method, the nation-state, development, and now adaptation emerged from specific European historical experiences yet are treated as universal frameworks all societies must adopt (Chakrabarty 2009; said1978?). Provincializing these concepts does not mean rejecting them entirely but recognizing their particularity and the violence involved in their universalization (spivak1988?; bhabha1994?).

This historical recognition opens space for recognizing that multiple valid knowledge systems exist and flourish across the globe. These knowledge systems offer fundamentally different ways of understanding environmental change, vulnerability, and appropriate responses (Santos 2016; berkes2012?). Indigenous knowledge systems may emphasize long-term observations of environmental patterns, reciprocal relationships with non-human beings, and adaptive strategies based on seasonal rhythms (berkes2012?; cajete2000?). Epistemological communities organized around place-based knowledge often focus on social relationships, mutual aid networks, and collective decision-making processes that have enabled survival through past environmental challenges (ostrom1990?; chambers1997?).

Yet contemporary governance frameworks systematically privilege certain forms of knowledge while marginalizing others. The concept of the “pluriverse” provides a framework for understanding this dynamic (Escobar 2018). The pluriverse contrasts the “one-world world” of modernity - which assumes a single reality that can be known through universal science - with recognition of multiple ontologies and ways of being that create genuinely different worlds (Escobar 2018; law2004?). The one-world world eliminates pluriversal possibilities by recognizing only certain forms of vulnerability, temporalities, and responses as legitimate.

Understanding how epistemicide operates requires examining the mechanisms through which expert communities achieve and maintain dominance. Expert communities claim authority by positioning themselves as objective, rational, and scientific (jasanoff2004?; porter1995?). This claim depends on distinguishing expert knowledge from other forms of knowing, which become categorized as subjective, particularistic, or merely traditional (gieryn1983?; shapin1985?). The process creates hierarchies where expert knowledge appears as knowledge proper while alternatives become something less - opinion, belief, folklore, or ignorance (said1978?; spivak1988?).

Professional socialization within expert communities reinforces these hierarchies through institutional arrangements that shape how members understand their role and authority (bourdieu1988?). Expert communities are trained in particular institutions, using standardized methodologies, reading similar literatures, and participating in professional networks that reinforce certain ways of seeing (collins1985?). When expert communities encounter phenomena that cannot be understood through their frameworks, the problem lies with the phenomena rather than the framework - they become inefficient, traditional, illegal, or simply wrong (Scott 1998).

Professional socialization creates strong incentives for conformity within expert communities. Members learn to be understood by their peers and to avoid sticking their necks out too much (kuhn1962?). Expert communities develop coherence through mutual reinforcement rather than critical examination of their own assumptions (knorr-cetina1999?). This creates epistemological communities that maintain stability through groupthink rather than openness to alternative perspectives (janis1972?).

The practical operation of epistemological dominance occurs through “technical rendering” - the transformation of complex realities into simplified representations that can be managed through expert tools (Li 2007). Technical rendering operates through abstraction, quantification, and standardization, creating what appears to be objective knowledge while systematically excluding aspects of reality that cannot be captured through these processes (porter1995?). This contrasts with approaches that recognize complexity and specificity through detailed, contextual understanding (geertz1973?).

Technical rendering serves multiple functions for expert communities. It makes complex realities governable by reducing them to manageable categories and metrics (rose1999?). It legitimates expert intervention by demonstrating technical competence and scientific objectivity (jasanoff2004?). Most importantly, it transforms political questions into technical problems that can only be solved by expert communities (Ferguson 1994). When vulnerability becomes a matter of exposure indices rather than power relations, when resilience becomes a matter of institutional capacity rather than social solidarity, the conditions that create problems become invisible while technical solutions appear both necessary and sufficient (wisner2004?).

Bureaucratic forms and funding criteria enforce this technical rendering by requiring conformity to expert frameworks (hull2012?). Documents must conform to particular formats, use specific vocabularies, and demonstrate competence in expert categories to be considered legitimate (riles2006?). Epistemological communities seeking resources must translate their understanding of environmental challenges into the technical language required by institutions (tsing2005?). This translation process systematically filters out aspects of knowledge that cannot be captured in expert categories (strathern2000?).

The result is “cognitive injustice” - the systematic devaluation of knowledge systems that do not conform to dominant paradigms (Santos 2016). Cognitive injustice operates not simply through exclusion but through transformation and appropriation (spivak1988?). Alternative knowledge systems are incorporated into expert frameworks in ways that strip them of their epistemological foundations (agrawal1995?). Indigenous knowledge becomes “climate indicators.” Community-based practices become “participatory assessment.” Social relationships become “social capital.” The transformation appears inclusive while actually operating as epistemological appropriation (cooke2001?).

Expert networks play a crucial role in maintaining epistemological dominance across different contexts (Brinks and Donner 2025). These networks - trained in similar institutions, using similar frameworks, participating in similar professional circuits - create corridors through which certain forms of knowledge flow while others are blocked (bourdieu1988?). Consultants working across multiple contexts create patterns of convergence, functioning as carriers of expert knowledge that marginalizes other ways of knowing (mosse2005?). The same technical assistance providers, using the same assessment tools, produce similar documents regardless of context.

The temporal dimensions of epistemological control reveal deep connections to anticipatory governance. Expert communities privilege linear temporality, predictive capacity, and future control (adam1998?). Alternative epistemological communities may emphasize cyclical time, adaptive capacity, and emergent futures (cajete2000?). When planning frameworks privilege expert temporality, they eliminate not just alternative forms of knowledge but alternative relationships to time itself (Escobar 2018).

Understanding these dynamics reveals how expert-driven processes may systematically eliminate alternatives while appearing to be inclusive and participatory (Li 2007). The mechanisms operate through seemingly neutral technical processes - assessment frameworks, planning templates, funding criteria - that quietly exclude ways of knowing that cannot conform to expert requirements (hull2012?). When epistemological communities cannot articulate their understanding in expert terms, they become excluded from planning processes that claim to represent their interests (Chakrabarty 2009). The following section examines how epistemological control and temporal control combine to create comprehensive mechanisms for managing both knowledge and futures.

4.3 A Theory of Control

This section synthesizes anticipatory governance and epistemological analysis to develop a theory of control that explains how expert communities manage both knowledge and futures through seemingly neutral technical processes that eliminate political alternatives while appearing to be inclusive and participatory.

The previous sections reveal how anticipatory governance and epistemological dominance operate as interconnected mechanisms of control. Anticipatory governance transforms political questions about desired futures into technical problems requiring expert management. Epistemological dominance ensures that only certain ways of knowing can inform these technical solutions. Together, these mechanisms create comprehensive systems for managing both knowledge and futures through processes that appear neutral while systematically excluding alternatives.

The central insight is that planning processes do not simply respond to challenges but actively create the futures they claim to predict (austin1962?). When expert communities develop plans to continue extracting oil, they end up extracting oil. When adaptation plans emphasize technological solutions and market mechanisms, they create worlds organized around technology and markets. The future becomes “acted” rather than predicted, making planning documents into blueprints for particular kinds of worlds rather than neutral assessments of likely developments (callon1998?).

This performative dimension operates through the systematic transformation of political questions into technical problems (Ferguson 1994). When vulnerability becomes a matter of exposure indices rather than power relations, when resilience becomes institutional capacity rather than social solidarity, the conditions that create problems disappear while technical solutions appear both necessary and sufficient (Li 2007). This transformation eliminates space for political mobilization around structural causes while legitimating continued intervention by expert communities (Escobar 1995).

Professional socialization within expert communities reinforces these control mechanisms through incentives for conformity and peer recognition (kuhn1962?). Expert communities learn to avoid sticking their necks out too much, developing coherence through mutual reinforcement rather than critical examination of assumptions (collins1985?). The result is epistemological communities that maintain stability through groupthink while appearing to operate through objective scientific inquiry (knorr-cetina1999?).

The control mechanisms create a peculiar dynamic where epistemological communities must engage in “adaptation to adaptation” - adapting their understanding to funding requirements rather than addressing environmental change (tsing2005?). Countries learn to speak the language of international climate institutions rather than articulating their own understanding of challenges and responses (hull2012?). This produces documents that serve institutional needs while potentially failing those most vulnerable to environmental change (riles2006?).

Bureaucratic forms and expert networks enforce these control mechanisms across different contexts (mosse2005?). The same technical assistance providers, using the same assessment tools, produce similar documents regardless of context (Lewis and Mosse 2006). Alternative knowledge systems are incorporated into expert frameworks in ways that strip them of their epistemological foundations, with indigenous knowledge becoming “climate indicators” and community practices becoming “participatory assessment” (agrawal1995?). The transformation appears inclusive while actually operating as epistemological extraction (cooke2001?).

This theory of control illuminates how expert communities may systematically eliminate alternatives while appearing to be inclusive and responsive (Li 2007). The mechanisms operate through seemingly neutral technical processes that quietly exclude ways of knowing and being that cannot conform to expert requirements (hull2012?). The result is the systematic production of singular worlds while pluriversal possibilities are rendered invisible, illegitimate, or simply unthinkable (Escobar 2018).
